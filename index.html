Komal Sah

Quantum dynamical systems · Computational physics · Scientific machine learning

I study complexity in open quantum systems and multistable stochastic dynamical systems, and develop measures to quantify it.

An isolated qubit traces a circle on the Bloch sphere. Once it interacts with other qubits, it is described by a cloud of states instead, one for each measurement outcome on the others. I ask two questions about that cloud:

  • Sensitivity: how quickly do nearby starting states become distinguishable?
  • Exploration: how much of the sphere does the cloud visit over time?

Formally, the cloud is a probability measure on state space, and I use optimal transport to compare measures. The same framework applies to both.

I completed my Ph.D. in Physics at the University of California, Davis in 2026, advised by James P. Crutchfield.

Research

My dissertation develops a measure-theoretic framework for stability, complexity, and state-space exploration in stochastic and open quantum dynamical systems.

Geometric diagnostics of open quantum dynamics

A reduced density matrix reproduces every measurement on a subsystem, but it discards how the subsystem's pure states are distributed. I represent subsystem states as probability measures on projective Hilbert space, called geometric quantum states, which keep that ensemble structure.

  • Introduced two complementary diagnostics: one for sensitivity to initial-state preparation and one for long-time exploration of quantum state space.
  • Applied the framework to the quantum kicked top, identifying how interaction strength, environment size, and finite-size parity structure affect dynamical complexity.
  • Built reusable Python workflows for quantum evolution, ensemble construction, optimal-transport calculations, and visualization.

Stochastic dynamics and optimal transport

Noise changes what stability means: a noisy system is described by a distribution, and the useful question is how distributions move. I use optimal transport to characterize stability, uncertainty growth, mixing, escape, and transport in noisy and multistable dynamical systems.

  • Designed numerical methods for perturbing and evolving probability measures and for estimating Wasserstein-based sensitivity across dynamical regimes.
  • Investigated how stochastic forcing reorganizes attractors, transition pathways, and long-time distributions in nonlinear systems.

A chaotic tale of the quantum kicked top

A qubit on its own traces a circle. Give it company and the circle disappears. This is the story of what replaces it.

1 · The isolated case

What does a qubit do when left alone?

Not quiet contemplation. Put a spin in a constant field and it goes around in a circle, indefinitely. A classical spin does this, and so does a qubit.

Without interactions the two pictures agree. Interactions are where they part ways.

2 · The interacting case

What changes when it has company?

Interactions with other spins deform the periodic trajectory in the classical case. Quantum mechanically, interactions with other qubits entangle the qubit with them, and its state becomes mixed. A mixed state no longer resides on the Bloch sphere. It sinks inside.

To study interactions, we turn to the fascinating case of the kicked top, a model realized in several experiments.

Why the kicked top?

Short answer: chaos is fascinating, and quantum chaos is elusive. Some would say chimerical: quantum evolution preserves the overlap between states, so two of them never drift apart the way chaotic trajectories do.

Longer answer: the kicked top has a well-studied chaotic classical counterpart and a single knob, κ, that tunes it from regular to chaotic. It has been realized with cold atoms (Chaudhury et al., 2009), in NMR (Krithika et al., 2019), and on superconducting processors (Neill et al., 2016; Anand et al., 2024). Nothing below is specific to it. The same machinery applies to other Hamiltonians.

It is a foundational many-body model with a non-interacting part that generates rotations and an interacting part that generates twists:

H = H0 + HI

HKT = (π/2τ) Jy + (κ/2j) Jz2 ∑n∈ℤ δ(t − nτ)

Here J is the collective spin of the N qubits, with j = N/2. The first term rotates every spin by π/2 about the y axis in each period τ. The second kicks them once per period with an interaction of strength κ.

3 · The mixed-state description

The quantum quandary: how does the orbit change on interaction?

Interactions leave the qubit in a statistical mixture of pure states, which we call a mixed state. There are two ways of describing a mixed state: as a probability cloud over the Bloch sphere (Anza & Crutchfield, 2021), or by the average of that cloud, a point inside the Bloch sphere.

Where do the two descriptions come from?

We start with the state of all the qubits after they interact. The qubit of interest is denoted S and the other qubits E:

|ΨSE(t)⟩ = dS∑k=1dE∑j=1 ψkj(t) |sk⟩ ⊗ |ej⟩

After a little mathematical feng shui, with |ej⟩ the computational basis, this state can be written as

|ΨSE(t)⟩ = dE∑j=1 √λjE(t) |χjS(t)⟩ |ej⟩

where

λjE(t) = dS∑k=1 |ψkj(t)|2

and, for λjE(t) > 0,

|χjS(t)⟩ = 1√λjE(t) dS∑k=1 ψkj(t) |sk⟩

Mixed state: the reduced density matrix

Tracing over the environment gives the reduced density matrix, a point inside the Bloch sphere:

ρS(t) = dE∑j=1 λjE(t) |χjS(t)⟩⟨χjS(t)|

Mixed state: a geometric ensemble

The conditional pure states |χjS(t)⟩, each a point on the Bloch sphere, together with their probabilities λjE(t), form a probability cloud over the Bloch sphere. This is the mixed state as an ensemble:

{ λjE(t), |χjS(t)⟩ }dE​j=1

Nothing is lost: average the cloud and the density matrix comes back. Something is gained: the cloud carries an imprint of the environment. One caveat: the cloud depends on the basis in which the environment is measured. Here it is the computational basis.

The same construction appears in the literature on projected ensembles, where it is used to study how random-looking states emerge from chaotic dynamics (Cotler et al.; Ippoliti & Ho). Here we use it to follow the dynamics in time and to understand and quantify the complexity of the emergent dynamics.

4 · Quantifying dynamical complexity

How is the dynamical complexity quantified?

Classically, in the absence of interactions, the distance between two close initial states is preserved. In the presence of nonlinear interactions, the distance between them changes with time. This behavior forms the basis of the measure of classical chaos, the maximal Lyapunov exponent, which measures the rate at which two nearby trajectories separate. Sensitivity is one signature of dynamical complexity, and it is the first of the two we carry over to quantum systems.

Sensitivity as a measure of quantum complexity

To measure sensitivity for a qubit that interacts with other qubits, we have to deal with the mixed states from the previous section. Which of the two descriptions should we track?

The density matrix: a point inside the sphere. One option is to follow the Bures distance between the density matrices of two nearby initial states. The trouble is that interactions entangle the qubit and push its state toward the centre of the Bloch sphere, and near the centre every state is close to every other. The distance tends to shrink as interactions grow, not because the dynamics is simpler but because averaging hides the differences. The measure points the wrong way.

The probability cloud: points on the sphere. The other option is to follow the distance between two nearby probability clouds. The Wasserstein distance from optimal transport supplies one. It is the minimal cost needed to move one cloud onto the other. The cloud is made of pure states on the surface, so mixing alone does not pull two clouds together, and differences that the average would hide stay visible.

The distinguishability measure Γ is the time-averaged relative distance between two nearby clouds. It is zero for an isolated spin, whose rigid rotation keeps the distance fixed, positive when interactions drive the clouds apart, and negative when they draw together.

How far apart are two quantum states?

It depends on who is asked. Take two nearby states of several qubits and there are three reasonable ways to measure the distance between them. They give three different answers.

Ask the whole system. For two pure states of the full system, the natural distance is the Fubini–Study distance, the angle between them: dFS(Ψ, Φ) = arccos |⟨Ψ|Φ⟩|. It has one awkward property for anyone hunting for chaos. Unitary evolution preserves the overlap, so this distance never changes. Two states that start close stay exactly as close.

Ask one qubit, through its density matrix. Trace out the other qubits and compare the two reduced density matrices with the Bures distance, dB = √(2 − 2√F), where F is the fidelity between them. This distance can change in time. But each density matrix is an average, so the comparison ignores how each qubit's possible states are distributed.

Ask one qubit, through its cloud. To compare two clouds, ask how much work it takes to move one onto the other. Moving probability from one point to another costs the Fubini–Study distance between them, and the Wasserstein distance is the total cost of the cheapest plan.

Exploration

Sensitivity is local and short-term. The second question is global: over a long time, how much of the sphere does the cloud visit? The state-space coverage index answers by comparing the visited region with a uniform covering of the sphere. A related question in the projected ensemble literature is whether the cloud itself becomes uniform over the sphere, including in systems with symmetries (Varikuti & Bandyopadhyay).

Turn on trails. At κ = 0 the qubit visits only four points, because four quarter turns bring it back to the start. Raise κ and let it run.

What shapes complexity

Interaction strength, environment size and parity symmetry all leave their mark. Both measures generally grow with κ. Three qubits form a half-integer spin and four an integer spin, and in the paper integer-spin systems often show more sensitivity and coverage than half-integer ones.

Keep κ and the starting state fixed, switch between 3 and 4 qubits, and compare the trails after a few hundred kicks.

5 · Measurement on hardware

More than mathematical trickery

The geometric quantum state can be measured. Measure the environment qubits, perform tomography on the system qubit for each outcome, and repeat. Notebook 5 in Qaos implements the protocol as a quantum circuit for the kicked top. Projected ensembles have also been sampled experimentally, in a Rydberg-atom simulator (Choi et al., 2023).

How much of the geometry survives hardware noise?

A preview of upcoming work: the same three-qubit dynamics computed three ways. First exactly, then on IonQ's ideal simulator, then on IonQ's hardware-aware simulator, which adds a noise model of its trapped-ion devices.
